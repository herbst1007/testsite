From Edo to Higo

Two Gardens,One Samurai Lineage

In Tokyo, the Hosokawa lords left a quiet stroll garden beneath the Mejiro hills. Nearly 900 kilometers south, in their home domain of Higo, they shaped a landscape of spring water and a miniature Mount Fuji. Walk one, and the other begins to make sense.

Prologue

The Hosokawa Legacy

Most samurai houses are remembered for what they conquered. The Hosokawa are remembered for what they kept.

For centuries this family stood near the center of Japanese history, as generals, as governors and, above all, as guardians of culture. Where other warlords collected castles, the Hosokawa collected knowledge: of poetry, of tea, and of the quiet art of arranging water, stone and pine.

Hosokawa Yusai was a battle-hardened commander and also the foremost master of the Kokin Denju, the secret oral teachings on Japan's great classical anthology of poetry. When enemy forces besieged his castle in 1600, the imperial court stepped in to end the siege, unwilling to let that knowledge die with him.

His son Tadaoki, remembered by his tea name Sansai, was counted among the seven great disciples of the tea master Sen no Rikyu. Tadaoki's son Tadatoshi became lord of Higo, today's Kumamoto, in 1632, and the family governed the domain from Kumamoto Castle until the feudal system ended in 1871. It was Tadatoshi who welcomed the swordsman Miyamoto Musashi to Kumamoto, where Musashi spent his final years and wrote The Book of Five Rings.

Service to the shogun kept the lords in Edo for long stretches, and their taste for gardens went with them. Two of their gardens frame this journey: one in Tokyo, one in Kumamoto. Together they tell a single story.

Chapter I

The Tokyo ChapterHigo-Hosokawa Garden, Bunkyo City

A daimyo garden hidden in plain sight, a few minutes' walk from Waseda.

肥後細川庭園

Where the Mejiro plateau falls away toward the Kanda River, the ground breaks into springs. In the closing years of the shogunate this hillside became a residence of the Hosokawa lords of Higo, and from 1882 it was the family's principal home in Tokyo.

The garden they left is a pond-stroll garden built on that natural drop. The wooded slope serves as its mountain, and spring water threads down a shallow yarimizu stream, a device borrowed from the gardens of the Heian court, before settling into the pond.

Above the water stands Shoseikaku, a wooden pavilion that served the family as a study hall and, after a remodeling in the Taisho era, for a time as their home. Restored and reopened in 2016, it is open to visitors once more.

Next door, the Eisei Bunko Museum preserves the family's treasures, from armor and tea utensils to calligraphy and painting. Mejirodai is where the Hosokawa story begins.

Chapter II

The Kumamoto ChapterSuizenji Jojuen Garden, Kumamoto City

The masterpiece of the home domain, filled with water that began as rain on Aso.

水前寺成趣園

Soon after arriving in Higo, Hosokawa Tadatoshi found a place where clear water rose from the ground, and in the 1630s he built a temple and a teahouse beside it. His grandson Tsunatoshi, the third lord, expanded the grounds into the great stroll garden seen today and named it Jojuen, after a line by the Chinese poet Tao Yuanming about a garden that grows more delightful with every daily walk.

Laid out in the Momoyama manner, the garden unfolds as a sequence of views around a broad, spring-fed pond. Tradition holds that its path evokes the fifty-three stations of the Tokaido, the great highway out of Edo: a journey through the shogun's Japan, rendered in miniature at the far end of the country.

Designated a National Historic Site and Place of Scenic Beauty in 1929, Suizenji also holds Izumi Shrine, built in 1878 to honor the Hosokawa lords.

A Fuji of one's own

Tsukiyama, the sculpted mountain

Across the water rises a softly symmetrical grass mound whose silhouette is unmistakably that of Mount Fuji. Far from the real peak, a Kyushu lord could gaze on Japan's most revered mountain from his own teahouse, and so can you.

Water from Aso

A garden fed by the volcano

Rain that falls on the vast Aso caldera sinks into volcanic rock and travels slowly underground before it surfaces here, so clear that the pond floor seems within reach. Kumamoto draws almost all of its drinking water from the same source. Inside the garden, visitors still collect the "water of long life" that wells up at Izumi Shrine.

Kokin Denju-no-Ma

The pavilion of the secret poetry teachings

Just inside the main gate stands a thatched pavilion with an extraordinary past. It once stood in the Kyoto residence of Prince Hachijo Toshihito, younger brother of the emperor, and is said to be the very room where Hosokawa Yusai passed the Kokin Denju to the prince in 1600.

Moved to Suizenji in 1912 and restored in 2010, it now welcomes guests. Sit on the tatami, take a bowl of matcha with a Kumamoto sweet, and let the garden arrange itself before you.

Kumamoto's own tea school, Higo Koryu, has handed down Sen no Rikyu's procedures for some four hundred years, unaltered.

Interlude

Edo Refinement, Higo Grandeur

Same family, same principles, two very different moods. Both are pond-stroll gardens fed by spring water and shaped around a mountain of their own. What changes is the scale, and the light.

The Journey

From Tokyo to Kumamoto

Closer than it looks on the map. Leave Haneda after breakfast and you can be walking Suizenji's shoreline by early afternoon.

HNDTokyo Haneda
approx. 1 h 45 min
KMJAso Kumamoto Airport

  1. Fly

    Tokyo Haneda to Aso Kumamoto Airport takes about 1 hour 45 minutes, with ANA, JAL and Solaseed Air flights throughout the day.

  2. Ride

    Airport limousine buses reach central Kumamoto in about 50 minutes.

  3. Arrive

    Kumamoto's streetcars link the city center, Kumamoto Castle and the Suizenji Koen stop in around 20 minutes.

Flight times and schedules vary by season and carrier. Please check with the airline before you travel.
